## Dating

While it is true that we do not have autographed copies of original Rāmāyaṇa (as is the case for the vast majority of manuscripts from antiquity), scholars compare manuscripts from completely different regions of the subcontinent and are separated by centuries in order to reconstruct the original text.
This is known as the "Critical Edition" of the text.
With regard to the dating, scholars apply comparative philological analysis and linguistics to ascertain the approximate date.
Based on this analysis, the majority of the text was composed between the 5th and 2nd century BC. 
Some parts such as the Uttarakāṇḍa may've been added as late as the 2nd or 3rd century AD, with Kālidāsa and Bhavabhūti generally serving as the terminus anti quem for its composition.



## Manuscripts
Although it's true that a majority of surviving physical manuscript copies of the Rāmāyaṇa date from the 17th century and the earliest substantial manuscript copies of the Vālmīki-Rāmāyaṇa date back roughly to the 11th to 12th centuries AD, this is due to the fact that birch bark and palm leaf manuscripts don't survive very long in a humid/tropical climate.

This is not uncommon, e.g. the earliest substantial Pāḷi palm leaf manuscripts of the Buddhist canon date to around the 15-17th century AD, though the Buddhist canon exists in translation (Koreana Tripiṭaka) around the 13th century AD.

Likewise, the earliest substantial manuscripts of parvans of the Mahābhārata date to the 11th to 12th centuries AD.

The oldest surviving complete manuscript copy of the Iliad, Venetus A, dates to around the 10th century AD, and this is despite the climate being more favorable for preservation.

## Continuity
If you are referring to continuity, the Rāmāyaṇa has been continually recited in the Indian subcontinent for millennia and it has inspired countless works.

The same is not true of the Iliad and Odyssey in continental Europe.  
While the Aeneid remained popularity throughout Europe, the text of the Iliad and Odyssey was essentialy lost outside of Greece.

## Jātakas
Regarding Jātakas, the Buddhist Jātaka tales of the Pāḷi canon are clearly aware of the Rāmāyaṇa, though the Rāmāyaṇa does not know of the Jātaka tales.

The Dasaratha-Jātaka quotes from Vālmīki (see Jacobi below on "dasa vassasahassāni...").

In the Vessantara-Jātaka, Maddī (the wife of Vessantara and mother of Jāli and Kaṇhājinā) likens herself to Sītā:

avaruddhassāham bhariyā, rājaputtassa sirīmato  
tañ cāham nātimaññāmi, Rāmam Sītā 'vanubbatā  
"I am the wife of a banished, glorious prince.   
and I do not scorn him [for I am] faithful just as Sītā was to [banished] Rāma.

In the Jayaddisa-Jātaka, Prince Alinasatta's (or Alinasattu's) family members offer saccakiriyās or vows/prayers for his protection against the purisādaka (puruṣādaka, "man eater").

- Alinasatta's father Jayaddisa calls upon Varuṇa, Soma, Pajāpati (Prajāpati), Candimā (Candramas), and Sūriya (Sūrya) to protect him.  
- Alinasatta's sister vows that she has never harbored ill will towards her brother, neither openly nor in secret.
- Alinasatta's wife vows that she has never been disloyal to him and that he has never been disagreeable to her mind.

However, his mother alludes to the "sotthāna" (Skt. svastyayana) or blessing given by Kausalyā to Rāma before his departure to Daṇḍakāraṇya:

> yaṃ daṇḍakirañño gatassa mātā rāmassakāsi sotthānaṃ suguttā  
taṃ te ahaṃ sotthānaṃ karomi, etena saccena sarantu devā  
anuññāto sotthi paccehi putta.

> The blessing which Rāma’s well-protected mother performed for him when he went to Daṇḍaka.  
That same blessing I perform for thee.  
By this truth, let the Gods remember [thee].
Having taken leave, return safely, O son!

This blessing of Kausalyā starting with "svasti sādhyāś ca viśve ca marutaś ca maharṣayaḥ" is not found anywhere in the Pāḷi canon.

It is found in the Ayodhyākāṇḍa of VR.
